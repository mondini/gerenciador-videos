## Bloco 2 — O lembrete (aprovado)

```
[thoughtful] In ancient Rome, there was no greater honor than a triumph.
A general came home from a great victory... and the whole city poured into the streets to see him.
He rode in a chariot, dressed almost like a god. Crowds. Trumpets. Captives marching behind.
[softly] And yet, an old writer tells us... a voice behind him kept whispering in his ear.
Look behind you. Remember... you are only a man.

[thoughtful] Today, the triumph looks different.
The record quarter. The contract you chased for a whole year. The team standing up to applaud... your name on the screen.
[softly] And no one is whispering behind you.
No one says... remember.

[thoughtful] Marcus Aurelius had no one to whisper either. He was the emperor. Who would dare?

Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee.
[warmly] In simple words... you don't have forever.
And then, in the same breath, he told himself what to do about it:
while you live... while you still can... be good.

[thoughtful] That is what memento mori really is.
Not a skull on the desk. Not a fear.
A filter.
Put your death next to your calendar... and watch what falls away.
The urgent things start to look small.
And that small voice from the other room... starts to look urgent.
[softly] The general needed the whisper for one day of glory.
Most of us need it... on an ordinary Tuesday.
```

## Bloco 3 — A vida que a gente adia (aprovado)

```
[thoughtful] Long before Marcus, another Roman watched the richest men of his city... and noticed something strange.
His name was Seneca.
These men guarded their money like soldiers guard a wall. No one could take a single coin.
[softly] But their time... they handed it out to anyone who asked.
A meeting. A favor. A dinner they never wanted to attend.
We are not short of days, Seneca said. We just waste them.

[thoughtful] And everywhere he went, he heard the same promise.
After fifty, I'll slow down. At sixty, I'll finally rest.
His answer was almost cruel.
Who promised you fifty?
[softly] How late it is, he wrote, to begin living... just when it's time to leave.

[thoughtful] Then he told the story of a man he knew.
Cornelius Senecio. He started with nothing... and built a fortune.
He knew how to make money, and how to keep it. He lived simply. He took care of his health.
One morning, he came to visit Seneca, as he always did.
He spent the rest of the day at the bedside of a sick friend.
In the evening, he had a good dinner.
And that night... his throat began to close.
[softly] He barely lived to see the sunrise.
[thoughtful] Seneca wrote that he died in the middle of his success... with money still pouring in. Investments on land and at sea.
[softly] Not one of his plans had failed.
He just ran out of tomorrow.

[thoughtful] Seneca drew one lesson from it:
Let us postpone nothing. Let us balance life's account every day.
[warmly] Any business owner understands that.
You don't wait until the end of the year to find out the company was losing money.
So why wait until the end of your life... to find out what you were spending it on?
```

## Bloco 4 — As crianças (aprovado)

```
[thoughtful] Now... let me tell you something about Marcus that most people never hear.
He was the most powerful man in the world. The best doctors of the empire answered to him.
And it did not matter.
[softly] As he was preparing to leave for war... his little son fell ill.
His name was Verus. He was seven years old.
The doctors cut into a swelling just below his ear... and the boy did not survive.
Seven years.
[thoughtful] The old historian tells us Marcus mourned him for only five days.
And even then... the empire kept knocking at his door. And he answered.
Then he went to war.

[softly] We don't know what he felt at night, in the tent, far from home.
But in his journal, there is one line he copied from the philosopher Epictetus.
It may be the hardest line in all of Stoicism.
When a man kisses his child... he should whisper to himself, "Tomorrow perchance thou wilt die."
[thoughtful] Someone objected. Those are words of bad omen.
And Epictetus said no. It is no more a bad omen... than saying the wheat will be harvested.
[softly] In simple words... the child in your arms is not yours forever.
Nothing is.

[thoughtful] It sounds unbearable. But listen again.
It doesn't say love them less.
It says... look at them. Now. While they're here.
[softly] Your son. Your daughter. Your niece, your nephew. The grandchild who calls you on Sundays.
One day, one of you will hug the other... for the last time.
And neither of you will know it.

[thoughtful] That is the whole of memento mori.
Not fear.
Attention.
[warmly] So the next time a small voice says, "Watch this"...
put everything down. And watch.

[softly] Let's pause here.
```

## Bloco 5 — Provérbio-ponte (aprovado)

```
[thoughtful] Centuries before Seneca, the book of Proverbs put this whole lesson into a single line.
Don't boast about tomorrow; for you don't know what a day may bring.
[softly] It doesn't say don't plan. Planning is wise.
It says don't boast. Don't act as if tomorrow already belongs to you.
Senecio's investments were a boast about tomorrow.
And so is "next summer."

[thoughtful] The same book has a quieter line... and it cuts even deeper.
Don't tell your neighbor, "Come back tomorrow, I'll give it to you then"... when you have it with you now.
[warmly] It was written about what we owe the people around us.
And what the people we love are asking for... is rarely money.
It's an afternoon. A trip. An hour on the floor with your kids.
You're not waiting until you can afford it.
You can already afford it.
[softly] You're only waiting... for tomorrow.
And tomorrow was never promised to anyone.
Not to a rich man in Rome.
Not to an emperor.
Not to you.
```

## Bloco 6 — Fechamento (aprovado)

```
[softly] In the year 180, on the frontier, with his army around him... Marcus Aurelius was dying.
His friends stood by his bed, weeping.
And he asked them... why are you crying for me? Think of the plague. Think of the death that waits for all of us.
[thoughtful] Near the end, he let only his son come in.
And then he sent him away... afraid his son would catch the sickness.
[softly] Then an officer came to ask for the watchword of the day.
And the emperor answered:
Go to the rising sun. I am already setting.
[thoughtful] That night, he covered his head... as if he were going to sleep.
And he did not wake up.

[softly] He didn't beg for more time.
He had spent his whole life... remembering he wouldn't have it.
[thoughtful] The rising sun was the ones who would come after him.
And yours... may be asleep in the next room right now.

[warmly] So tonight, before you sleep, try something Seneca suggested.
Close your eyes... and say quietly: I have lived.
Not because everything is done. Because today was real.
[softly] And if tomorrow comes... take it as a gift.
Hug them a little longer. Take the trip.
While it's still today.

[softly] Old wisdom... for quiet hours.
```
